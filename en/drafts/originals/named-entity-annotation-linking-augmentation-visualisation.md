## Lesson overview

This tutorial presents a workflow for curating a dataset of named entities in textual corpora to facilitate their exploration with computationally assisted text analysis and their publication as Linked Open Data (LOD). The lesson employs open-source tools and is designed to be accessible to humanities scholars new to digital methods: semi-automated annotation with Recogito and reconciliation and enrichment with OpenRefine using [Uniform Resource Identifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_Resource_Identifier) (URIs); creating new Wikidata items for unreconciled entities; preparing the dataset for network analysis with Gephi. The lesson can be complemented with the [introduction to LOD principles](https://programminghistorian.org/en/lessons/intro-to-linked-data) by Blaney and the methodology of building [a gazetteer of places from a historical corpus](https://programminghistorian.org/en/lessons/space-place-gazetteers) by Grunewald and Mostern.

## Introduction

### Linked entities in humanities texts

Named entities, such as people, places, works, and historical events, are constitutive elements of textual corpora in the humanities, and are often documented in the indexes of print and digital editions as well as in cultural heritage archives. Their digital curation as linked entities facilitates the exploration of their correlations computationally and, when published as LOD using the Resource Description Framework (RDF), enables their reuse across datasets on the Semantic Web. Digital Humanities projects are increasingly adopting graph data models with growing appreciation for the informational content of a text through the semantic annotation of named entities (Spadini et al 2021).[^1] As Vogeler elaborates (2019),[^2] the methodology for modeling historical texts as "assertive editions" begins with 

>"generic Natural Language Processing steps and then uses Named Entity Recognition to mark up the words representing people, locations, or organizations, temporal data, and quantifying data. The pipeline then relates these entities to one another, building connections between the entities”.

Named Entity Recognition (NER) and Entity-Linking (EL) support the identification and disambiguation of entities by associating textual references with stable identifiers (URIs) in knowledge bases, though automation remains challenging in the humanities due to few gold standard annotated corpora (Linhares Pontes et al. 2020).[^3] Manual and semi-automated annotation and linking of named entities remain essential in the curation of LOD datasets (Nugues 2024).[^4] Despite the proliferation of digital curation projects, humanities datasets largely lack interoperability (Brown and Simpson 2015;[^5] Hawkins 2021).[^6] The vision of the Semantic Web for “a decentralized database of intertextual relations throughout numerous online editions and archives” (Oberreither 2023:72)[^7] relies on broader scholarly participation across humanities domains. Whether we want to compile a dataset for digitally assisted text analysis, publish a digital edition of primary sources, map all the geographic locations mentioned in a text, or study an author’s sources and intertexts, the creation of LOD is fundamental to this research and editorial workflow. 

### The sample text and its source document: Montaigne’s _Essays_ online

An eclectic work, [Michel de Montaigne’s _Essays_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essays_%28Montaigne%29) are interspersed with quotations and references to historical and fictional persons and events, weaving a dense intertextual fabric. Although several scholarly digital projects have been dedicated to Montaigne, they haven't taken advantage of linked data for named entities.[^8] This lesson's dataset of linked entities of people and places to Wikidata in a selection of twenty of the *Essays* is a new contribution to Montaigne scholarship. Since knowledge bases like Wikidata are language-agnostic, the dataset can be integrated into existing projects and augmented by other editors. The sample text used in this tutorial is an English translation of the *Essays* downloaded from [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Essays_of_Montaigne), which was edited by its volunteer community based on an Optical Character Recognition (OCR) scan of Hazlitt's 1877 edition. In comparison with [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3600)--the other major community-curated digital library of texts in the public domain, Wikisource has the advantage of making available an individual document for each essay, which facilitates a modular, incremental approach to editing the corpus. Wikisource also provides a dynamic editing framework, allowing for transcription corrections to the source document in real time, and the possibility to enrich it with the linked entity dataset. 

## From Text to Entities: Named Entity Recognition and annotation with Recogito 

### Getting started with Recogito

[Recogito](https://recogito.pelagios.org/) was developed by the Pelagios network as an open-source semantic annotation platform to support the LOD curation of places. The annotation workspace has a graphical interface that enables individuals and teams to apply NER to places and people using Stanford CoreNLP, and to link places to several [gazetteers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gazetteer). The functionality of the Recogito workspace also includes manual annotation of entities and their relations, digital mapping, and export of the annotated data for further processing and publication. The Recogito website offers multilingual [tutorials](https://recogito.pelagios.org/help); for informative discussion of its application to specific case studies, see also Rio Riande (2020)[^9] and Rojas Castro (2025).[^10] Recogito is available as a hosted service by [registering](https://recogito.pelagios.org/signup) for a free user account, or you can download it from GitHub for personal use.

### Parse your document for places and people
In your dedicated workspace, documents can be uploaded in .txt UTF-8 or TEI format. Select a document to activate a menu of *Options*, where you can open, duplicate, delete, and organize documents, as well as apply Named Entity Recognition. When you open a document, the workspace provides a *Document view* for manual annotation, a *Map view* for geolocations, *Annotation statistics* for evaluation, and *Download Options* for exporting annotated data. To begin the NER annotation of places and people, select Named Entity Recognition from the *Options* menu and then choose the appropriate *Recognition engine* for parsing the text (in this case, the English language model of Stanford CoreNLP). All the gazetteers linked to Recogito are checked by default, but you can delimit the identification process by selecting only those relevant to the historical and geographical context of your corpus. 

### Evaluate the NER annotations and add manual annotations

The evaluation of tagged entities entails a degree of close reading, which depends on prior familiarity with the text, and may require consultation of secondary scholarship and additional research. In this workflow, close reading is the primary method of entity identification and interpretation. NER facilitates discovery of named entities, but it also produces a significant number of errors. Thus, in the sample document, Montaigne's essay "On Sorrow" (Book 1, Essay 2), the automated annotation identified 21 people and 13 places, which were revised to 30 people and 5 places, indicating missing entities and false positives. Recogito's NER models are currently limited to four Western languages and perform unevenly on multilingual and stylistically complex texts; its automated recognition therefore functions as a heuristic starting point for editorial close reading. Before proceeding with the manual evaluation, you could also repeat the NER with a different tool and compare the resulting entity annotations.[^11]

In the *Document view*, select the *annotation mode*, and use the *quick* mode to focus on either places or people, or the *normal* mode to highlight the annotations by entity type. As you click on each annotation, a popup window activates several options: deleting the annotation, changing its entity type (place, person, event), adding a custom tag or a comment. If the entity has been recognized as a place and linked to a gazetteer, you can also *Confirm* or *Change* the automatic match. The *Change* button shows a list of suggestions with a brief description and location on a map, which you can explore further by clicking on each id to connect to its gazetteer entry. If the correct suggestion is not listed, try modifying the spelling or adding context (e.g., the country to a town) in the search box.

#### Place annotations

In the sample text, the reference to the Hungarian city of Buda in the sixteenth century is tagged correctly as a place, but the suggested location from GeoNames is a town in Romania, despite Hungary being mentioned immediately before in reference to King John of Hungary.

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-01.png" alt="Recogito interface showing an automatically identified place on a map, its gazetteer identifier, and options to confirm, change, or delete the match." caption="Figure 1. Recogito: the automatic match for a place shows a map location, gazetteer id, and options to confirm, change or delete it." %}

To link the correct identity of the place, select *Change* to review the list of suggestions; in this case, the [Kima gazetteer](https://data.geo-kima.org/) correctly identifies the Hungarian city. After selecting it, the annotation will be tagged as verified, and this qualification will be included as a column in the exported CSV file. If you cannot find the correct entity in the available gazetteers, you can add a URI identified manually (for example, on [Wikidata](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:Main_Page)) or *Flag this place* to complete the reconciliation at a later stage with another tool, such as OpenRefine. After completing the NER vetting and manual annotation, the *Map view* can be used to verify geolocations before exporting the data. 

#### Person annotations

In Montaigne’s _Essays_, quotations in multiple languages and their English translations result in erroneous annotation of some words (“Lingua” as a person and “Labitur” as a place), which should be deleted. Conversely, some entities are not recognized, notably the ancient Greek author Sophocles, who must be tagged manually (*+Person*). By contrast, the Roman author Ovid is misidentified as a place—Ovid Township in Michigan—and must be deleted and re-tagged as a person. 

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-02.png" alt="Recogito interface showing the name 'Ovid' incorrectly annotated as a place, linked to a GeoNames entry for Ovid Township in Michigan, with a map of that location." caption="Figure 2. Recogito: erroneous annotation of the poet Ovid as a place." %}

It is common to encounter references to people expressed through pronouns or periphrasis. In the sentence mentioning King John of Hungary and Buda (see Figure 1), Recogito tags “John” as a person and “Hungary” as a place, although Hungary is part of John's title, and the entire reference is to “the widow of King John of Hungary”. Her name (Isabella Jagiellon) can be recorded in the *Add a comment* field, facilitating later reconciliation with Wikidata in OpenRefine. 

As annotations are modified or created, Recogito recognizes other occurrences of the same character sequence and prompts re-application, which should be evaluated on an individual case basis. When creating a new annotation, highlight only the relevant text, as leading punctuation (e.g. “—Seneca”) may be handled correctly in the interface but converted into a question mark in the CSV export.  

#### Event annotations

Recogito has a third tag used for marking events, which is not generated through NER and must be added manually. In the sample text, the defeat of Cannae refers to the [Battle of Cannae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Cannae), where Cannae is already automatically recognized as a place and is correctly linked to its entry in the [Pleiades](https://pleiades.stoa.org/home) gazetteer.

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-03.png" alt="Recogito interface showing the place 'Cannae' linked to a Pleiades gazetteer entry with a brief description and a map of its location" caption="Figure 3. Recogito: automatic identification of Cannae as a place, linked to its Pleiades gazetteer entry." %}

To mark the event, highlight the relevant text and click *+Event*. The event tag is partially superimposed on the place tag, allowing the same string to be represented as both a place and an event in the exported dataset. 

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-04.png" alt="Recogito document view showing overlapping annotations on the same text fragment as both a place (highlighted in green) and an event (highlighted in purple)." caption="Figure 4. Recogito: overlapping annotations of the same text fragment as both a place (green) and an event (purple)." %}

#### Custom tags and comments

If additional entity types are relevant, custom tags, such as "work" and "quote", can be added manually. In this tutorial, the primary entity types curated are persons and places; examples of custom tags for works are included here as an optional extension rather than as a comprehensive layer. For example, Ovid’s _Metamorphoses_, which appears as the abbreviation “Met.” in editorial parentheses in the print edition, is tagged incorrectly as a place. After deleting the place tag, the custom tag “work” can be applied to identify the entity as a creative work. Custom tags assigned to text selections are stored in the *Add tag* menu for reuse across the document. A comment spelling out the title can be added to identify the _Metamorphoses_ as the source of Montaigne’s quote by using the *Add a comment* field. In cases where names or titles are supplied by the editor rather than present in the original text, a comment labeled "notes" can be used to distinguish editorial interventions from authorial references. When applied comprehensively, such distinctions clarify which authors and works Montaigne names explicitly and which he quotes without reference, based on editorial additions in the print edition.

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-05.png" alt="Recogito annotation workspace showing a custom tag and a comment added to a text selection to identify a creative work." caption="Figure 5. Recogito: adding a custom tag and comments to identify a creative work." %}

#### Entity relations

Semantic relations between two entities, such as between the author and the work or between a quote and its source, can be added in the *Document view*. Relations are represented visually by directional arrows indicating their semantic orientation. For example, "isAuthorOf", "hasAuthor", or "isPartOf" can be adopted from the Resource Description Framework (RDF) to describe relationships between bibliographic entities on the Semantic Web.

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-06.png" alt="Recogito document view showing semantic relations represented by dotted lines with arrows, with one arrow tagged 'isPartOf' going from a quote annotation to a work annotation, and another tagged 'isAuthorOf' going from an annotated person entity to an annotated work entity" caption="Figure 6. Recogito: annotating a relation between a work and its author and between a quote and its source." %}

#### Exporting and preparing annotated data for reconciliation

Since our dataset includes both places and persons, Recogito functions primarily as an annotation environment supporting NER, whose main output is a set of CSV files encoding entity occurrences, which we will reconcile comprehensively and enrich in OpenRefine. The exported annotation data should be sorted in a spreadsheet and separated into distinct CSV files by entity type, i.e. separate for persons and for places. For the next stage of data curation, only the entity label, entity type, any associated comments or tags, and existing URIs need to be retained. Normalize any spelling variations by re-sorting the list alphabetically and comparing the records; duplicate entities should be retained, as they are required for eventual statistical analysis and network visualization frequencies. To maintain a reproducible workflow, keep exported files clearly named by tool. Because each transformation is tool-specific and documented, the workflow remains transparent without requiring formal version control systems.

## From Entities to Linked Data: knowledge base reconciliation and enrichment of named entities in OpenRefine

[OpenRefine](https://openrefine.org/) is an open-source desktop application with a browser interface. This tutorial uses Version 3.9.3 for knowledge base reconciliation of the dataset; if data normalization is still necessary beforehand, the software [documentation](https://openrefine.org/docs) and the tutorial [Cleaning Data with OpenRefine](https://programminghistorian.org/en/lessons/cleaning-data-with-openrefine) provide guidance. At this stage, the objective is to link the list of person names to their corresponding entries in Wikidata. The same process can be followed to reconcile the list of place names, which Recogito does not accomplish comprehensively. In this tutorial's dataset, both people and place names were reconciled with Wikidata, in addition to the Recogito gazetteers.
As an openly editable and community-maintained resource, Wikidata allows scholars to reuse existing identifiers and to contribute machine-readable descriptions of their research domains where gaps exist. Recent publications in the _Journal of Open Humanities Data_ illustrate how Wikidata can support the integration and reuse of humanities datasets in semantically interoperable workflows.[^12]

To get started with OpenRefine, select the *Create project* tab to upload the CSV file of the list of people, and then select *Next*. A preview screen appears prompting you to configure parsing options, but OpenRefine usually detects the correct format automatically; in the sample dataset, row 1 is correctly identified as containing the column headers “person name” and “comments”. Once the project loads, go to the column labeled *person name* and click the downward arrow. From the dropdown menu, select *Facet* and then *Text facet*. This creates a *Facet* panel on the left, listing all unique text values in that column and their counts. Facets allow you to group your data and, together with filters, support inspection and selective exclusion during reconciliation. If you decide to exclude certain records or categories in the process of reconciliation, note that while columns can be removed directly from their dropdown menus, rows must be excluded using filters. To do so, go to the *All* column and mark the rows you want to delete with stars or flags and select the corresponding facet value. OpenRefine then sorts the data into *true* and *false* values, where *true* corresponds to the starred or flagged rows. From the dropdown menu of the *All* column, select *Edit rows* and *Remove matching rows*. Note that OpenRefine tracks every editing step in the *Undo/Redo* panel next to the *Facet/Filter* panel, where you can resume editing from an earlier step.

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-07.png" alt="OpenRefine table displaying a column of person names with a text facet panel on the left listing unique values and frequency counts" caption="Figure 7. OpenRefine: a list of person names defined with a text facet." %}

### Reconcile a list of names with Wikidata

Your next step is to create a new column that duplicates the list of names and stores the reconciliation results separately from the source data. From the *person name* column, open the dropdown menu and select *Edit column*. Then from the second dropdown menu select *Add column based on this column*. In the popup window, write “Wikidata” in the *New column name* box. You can now use this column to begin the reconciliation process: click the downward arrow to select the option *Reconcile* and from the next dropdown menu select *Start reconciling*. OpenRefine takes you to a screen to select a reconciliation service, where the default option is Wikidata. There, you also have the option to connect additional reconciliation services, such as the Virtual International Authority File (VIAF), or the Getty Vocabularies, which provide stable authority identifiers but do not support the same degree of open-ended semantic modeling and scholarly contribution as Wikidata. Indeed, these identifiers are often aggregated by Wikidata and are therefore best used as complementary reference points. 

After selecting the Wikidata reconciliation service, a configuration window prompts you to choose an entity class. OpenRefine will detect a class based on your data (in this case “human”), which is selected by default. If your dataset has mixed classes, you could reconcile without specifying one, but the suggestions will be less accurate. To refine reconciliation results, you can include data from other columns, for example when the “comments” column includes attributes such as “author” or “mythological figure”. OpenRefine enables the option to auto-match high confidence candidates by default. It is best to leave this option selected, since all automatic matches will be reviewed afterwards, just as they were for place names in Recogito. When the reconciliation completes, OpenRefine displays a partially colored bar below the “Wikidata” column header, showing the percentage of automated matches with high confidence (27% in this dataset). These are activated as dark blue links to the corresponding Wikidata page, while entries with suggested matches are displayed as light blue links. If you hover over a link, a brief description from Wikidata appears, and clicking the link will open the full Wikidata entry in a new tab. With the hover-over feature, it is possible to quickly verify that Diodorus Siculus, Pliny the Elder, Ovid, Isabella Jagiellon, among others, are auto-matched correctly. 

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-08.png" alt="OpenRefine table of person names showing a reconciled Wikidata column with a progress bar, where high-confidence matches appear as dark blue links and suggested matches appear as light blue links." caption="Figure 8. OpenRefine: reconciled person names distinguishing high-confidence matches from suggested matches." %} 

The record “Lesbia”, which in Montaigne's essay appears in a quote by the poet Catullus, is also automatically matched to the Wikidata item “Clodia”. Although this is correct insofar as Catullus used the pseudonym “Lesbia” to refer to the historical Clodia, the context here refers to her fictional role. The match can be refined by selecting *Choose new match* and then *Search for match*. The results now include matches of different class types, and the first suggestion is “Lesbia” described as “lover of Catullus”. Viewing this item on Wikidata shows that it includes the semantic property “said to be the same as,” linking it to the item for Clodia and illustrating how linked data can support semantic disambiguation and contextual specificity. 

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-09.png" alt="OpenRefine 'Search for match' window displaying candidate entities from multiple classes, with 'Lesbia, lover of Catullus' listed as the top result." caption="Figure 9. OpenRefine: refining reconciliation by searching across multiple entity classes." %}

The unreconciled entities may have a ranked list of potential candidates or no matches altogether. Resolving ambiguous or unmatched cases may require returning to the textual context, consulting secondary scholarship, or conducting original research. In this dataset, “Dionysius the Tyrant” and “Ferdinand I” each return multiple potential matches (see Figure 8 above), while no suggestions are provided for "Psammenitus, King of Egypt". In the case of Dionysius the Tyrant, textual context (described as “who died of joy”) identifies him as Dionysius I, tyrant of Syracuse, the third option on the list. OpenRefine allows reconciliation of either a single cell or all identical cells; here, all occurrences are matched, since the identity is consistent across the text and editorial commentary. For Ferdinand I, the initial suggestions are incorrect, but the correct match appears under *See more* as Ferdinand I, Holy Roman Emperor, historically in military conflict with Isabella Jagiellon. In cases where no correct match is initially available, *Search for match* can be used to retrieve candidates from different entity classes, as in the case of "Iphigenia", which in this context refers to a mythological figure rather than to the historical persons suggested by default.  

Sometimes spelling variations or aliases prevent correct identification and it may be necessary to modify the original record in the search box. For example, no suggestions appear for “Psammenitus, King of Egypt,” because the name is more commonly rendered as “Psamtik”. Searching for “Psamtik” successfully retrieves several Egyptian pharaohs by that name, allowing identification of Psamtik III based on historical context, which in turn helps disambiguate the identity of related figures such as Cambyses, King of Persia. 

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-10.png" alt="OpenRefine reconciliation interface showing a manual search for a name's alias in the 'Search for match' field." caption="Figure 10. OpenRefine: modifying the text record alias to search for a Wikidata item." %}

The only suggestion for "Cambyses" in both the initial list of matches as well as in *Search for match* is "Cambyses I". In such cases, you need to enter the correct historical person in the search box, namely "Cambyses II". If a Wikidata item exists, it will be retrieved, and once the match is selected the Wikidata column will update from “Cambyses, King of Persia” to “Cambyses II”.

If the name search fails to retrieve the correct entry or does not return any results, try searching for it directly in [Wikidata](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:Main_Page). Note that Wikidata items are identified by Q-identifiers (QIDs) which serve as machine-readable URIs. If you find a match, copy its QID and paste it into the search box in OpenRefine, and the corresponding entity will be retrieved. 

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-11.png" alt="OpenRefine reconciliation interface showing a Wikidata QID entered directly into the search field to retrieve a specific entity." caption="Figure 11. OpenRefine: linking an entity by adding its Wikidata QID in the *Search for match* field." %}

### Create new Wikidata items

Creating new Wikidata items in selected cases allows scholars and students to make their research objects visible and reusable beyond a single project, contributing structured knowledge to a shared, public infrastructure. If you cannot match certain entities to existing items in Wikidata, you may consider creating new Wikidata items for them. In our sample, no item exists for “Raïsciac, a German lord,” whom Montaigne reports to have died of grief upon seeing the body of his son who was killed in a battle near Buda during the conflict between Ferdinand I and Isabella Jagiellon. The text provides enough contextual information to create a structured Wikidata item for this individual. First, you should create a Wikidata account by registering a username and password. Before contributing, it is advisable to familiarize yourself with the structure of Wikidata items, especially those corresponding to the type of entity you want to create. For instance, the entry for [Psamtik III](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Q316278) includes a description, aliases, multilingual labels, and a set of structured statements describing attributes such as dates, gender, political affiliation, occupation, and manner of death, each expressed through properties (P-identifiers) with values typically drawn from other Wikidata items (Q-identifiers). These properties can be exported from Wikidata after the reconciliation to enrich your dataset. Below the list of *Statements*, a section of *Identifiers* links this item to external authority resources, such as [*Encyclopædia Britannica*](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Psamtik-III).

To create a Wikidata item for Raïsciac, go to [Wikidata’s main page](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:Main_Page) and select *Create a new Item* from the menu. In the window that appears fill in the basic metadata: language, label, a short description. 

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-12.png" alt="Wikidata interface for creating a new item, showing input fields for language selection, label, short description, and aliases" caption="Figure 12. Wikidata: creating a new item by selecting a language field and entering a label and a short description" %}

Once you click *Create* the new item will be assigned a QID. Be careful when creating new items, since deletion requires submitting a request to Wikidata administrators. All modifications you make are recorded in the item's edit history and associated with your user profile or, if logged out, your IP address.

The new Wikidata item now has a QID (Q135004989) and you can begin to add statements describing the entity. These include its class, gender, country of citizenship, place and manner of death—attributes that are salient in Montaigne's narrative. The first statement asserts instance of "human", which is given the reference *stated in* with the value *Montaigne’s Essays* (Q530157) and additional references listing the URLs for digital editions and facsimiles of the text. Another relevant statement uses the property *described by source*, again the value *Montaigne’s Essays*, to which you can *add qualifier* specifying the relevant section (Book I, essay 2 “On Sorrow”). Additional statements describe the person's gender ("male"), country of citizenship ("Holy Roman Empire"), occupation ("military personnel"), social classification ("noble"), place of death ("Buda"), and manner of death ("acute stress reaction"). Each of these statements requires explicit references, typically using *stated in* with *Montaigne’s Essays* as the source. For the date of death, which is not given explicitly in the text, qualifiers for “earliest date” and “latest date” can be added, and the statement should be referenced as "inferred from" rather than "stated in", since this timeframe is deduced from the historical context of the military conflict between Ferdinand I and Isabella Jagiellon (September 1540—August 1541). The live [Wikidata item](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Q135004989) allows to examine how evidence, qualifiers, and references are structured, revised, and debated over time. Once the narrative evidence has been translated into structured statements, the QID for Raïsciac can be added to our OpenRefine dataset, allowing properties from Wikidata to be imported for all reconciled entities.

### Extract QIDs and URLs from reconciled data

Once you have reconciled your data, you need to extract both QIDs and Wikidata URLs for each matched entity. QIDs are useful for disambiguating, sorting, aggregating, merging duplicates, and referencing entities internally (in networks or tables). Wikidata URLs are required for Linked Data and RDF export; they function as dereferenceable, machine-readable identifiers that connect the dataset to the Semantic Web. From the drop-down menu of the reconciled Wikidata column select *Reconcile* and then choose *Add column with URLs of matched entities*. You will be prompted to label the new column (e.g., "Wikidata URLs"), and then it will be added. Repeat the same steps to *Add entity identifiers column* and label this column "QIDs". The dataset now contains linked entities that can be exported as RDF or used for further semantic enrichment.

### Enrich the dataset with Wikidata properties 

We can proceed with extracting additional properties from the reconciled Wikidata column to enrich the dataset and expand the range of possible queries. From the dropdown menu of the reconciled column, select *Edit column* and choose *Add columns from reconciled values*. A window will appear with a short list of suggested properties, a box where you can search for a property (such as *date of death*), and a preview pane where you can evaluate whether selected properties offer useful values. You can also configure each property by selecting a different choice of ranks and references than the default options. For the sample dataset used in this tutorial, the values retrieved include date of birth, date of death, gender, occupation, and country of citizenship. After adding the new columns, you can remove, rearrange, and rename them through their dropdown menu. Since multiple values can be assigned to a given property (e.g., occupations, children, notable works), you should switch the project view from rows to records. Thus, the record for Ferdinand I, Holy Roman Emperor has three rows for occupation: aristocrat, monarch, and politician.

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-14.png" alt="OpenRefine dataset enriched with additional columns imported from Wikidata, including dates, gender, occupation, and country of citizenship, with multiple rows per entity." caption="Figure 14. OpenRefine: records in the sample dataset enriched with Wikidata properties." %}

We can now explore these properties quantitatively. For example, we can create a text facet for the “occupation” column which groups the data by counts, allowing to identify the most common occupations: poet (7), writer (7), politician (6), philosopher (5), historian (4), etc., out of the 40 different ones practiced by the 23 unique individuals mentioned in this essay. At this stage, the enriched dataset should be exported as a CSV file which will serve for the network visualizations in the next section.

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-15.png" alt="OpenRefine facet panel listing occupation values grouped by frequency counts, showing the distribution of occupations across the dataset." caption="Figure 15. OpenRefine: the Facet panel listing the occupations of person entities by count." %}

### Apply the RDF extension and export the dataset

After the reconciliation and enrichment of your dataset with Wikidata, you can export it as a CSV file to use in other applications for further analysis. To publish your dataset as linked data, it must first be modeled using RDF and semantic vocabularies. A recommended lesson at this stage is [Introduction to the Principles of Linked Open Data](https://programminghistorian.org/en/lessons/intro-to-linked-data). You can begin the modeling process by experimenting with the RDF extension in OpenRefine. First, install it by going to the main menu for creating a project and select the option *Extensions*, followed by *Discover extensions* to browse available plugins and download the RDF extension (rdf-extension-1.6.0). Unzip the folder and move it to the extensions directory of your OpenRefine application. The software [documentation](https://openrefine.org/docs/manual/installing#installing-extensions) gives detailed instructions for different operating systems. Once installed, the RDF extension appears in the OpenRefine workspace alongside other extensions (note that Wikibase is pre-installed by default). 

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-16.png" alt="File directory view showing the RDF extension folder placed inside the OpenRefine extensions directory on a Windows system." caption="Figure 16. The RDF extension folder added to the OpenRefine directory on a Windows system." %}

To begin modeling, click the dropdown menu of the RDF extension and select Edit *RDF skeleton* which opens a window for *RDF Schema alignment*. The RDF Schema alignment is a visual editor where you can assign semantic properties to the columns describing the attributes in the dataset. Select *Add property* to choose a term from the available prefixes (the names of semantic vocabularies and ontologies) that corresponds to the attribute in each column. For instance, you can assign `foaf:gender` to the column (*Cell*) labeled "sex or gender" extracted from Wikidata. FOAF is the acronym for "Friend of a Friend"—an ontology used to describe people and their relationships in RDF. When matching a semantic property to your list of columns, you will be prompted to define the cell's data type, i.e. gender is defined as text, so is occupation, date of birth and date of death are defined as date, Wikidata URL is defined as URI, etc.

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-17.png" alt="OpenRefine RDF Schema alignment window showing the property 'occupation' assigned to a dataset column." caption="Figure 17. OpenRefine: adding the property 'occupation' in the RDF Schema alignment." %}

The RDF skeleton feature of OpenRefine includes several preloaded vocabularies (e.g., `schema, foaf, rdfs, owl`), but you can import others from the [Linked Open Vocabularies](https://lov.linkeddata.es/dataset/lov/) (LOV) platform by clicking *+Add* next to the available prefixes and then typing the name and corresponding namespace URI of the desired vocabulary in the box that opens. For instance, you could add the MADS (Metadata Authority Description Schema) to model the property “occupation” in RDF.  

After aligning your data with the appropriate vocabulary terms, you can export the dataset in RDF Turtle format. Publishing the dataset as RDF makes the results of close reading interoperable and reusable beyond a single project, enabling integration with other linked datasets. OpenRefine’s RDF extension supports lightweight modeling and export; more complex ontology design, validation, or inspection can be carried out using dedicated RDF tools, depending on the goals of the project. 

## From Linked Data to Relations: named entity network analysis and visualization with Gephi

You can explore the enriched named entity dataset through network analysis and visualization that reveal relational patterns in humanities corpora which are challenging to discern through linear reading alone (cf. [R. Ahnert et al. 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/network-turn/CC38F2EA9F51A6D1AFCB7E005218BBE5)). In this tutorial, network visualization allows us to examine some aspects of how Montaigne’s discourse is structured through his historical and cultural references to persons and places. We will create a tripartite network of essays, people and places from the first five essays of each book. The network represents co-occurrences across the corpus of fifteen essays, revealing degrees of interconnection among entities and suggesting patterns in the formal organization of the text, while taking into account the dataset's limited selection. Although works have not been modeled as part of this dataset, they would introduce an important semantic layer of intertextual influence. Anchoring the network of entities in Wikidata augments its analytical potential by integrating additional properties into the dataset and by affording comparative analysis with other linked data corpora. 

### Setting up the dataset for network analysis with Gephi 

[Gephi](https://gephi.org/) is a well-documented, open-source desktop application for network visualization and analysis that provides interactive layouts and statistical measures by default, allowing users to explore relationships in their dataset without programming skills. To prepare the dataset for network analysis with Gephi, we need to create two CSV files: a nodes table listing each node of the network and its id (optionally, with columns for any attributes, such as gender, country, occupation, editorial note), and an edges table that defines relationships between the nodes. For this network, we are exploring the basic relationship between named entities and their occurrences in the selection from the *Essays* whose entities were reconciled in Wikidata. Thus, since Virgil is mentioned in Book I, Essay 2, this mention becomes an edge in the network, where Virgil is a node with type "person" and Book 1, Essay 2 is a node with type "essay". In the edges table, Book I Essay 2 is described as the “source” and Virgil as the “target”, even though this network of occurrences is undirected, since we are not modeling derivation or influence.

To set up the tables of nodes and edges from the CSV files exported from OpenRefine, we copy the Wikidata column with normalized person and place names for the labels and the corresponding QIDs column to serve as the unique identifiers for these nodes. Since Wikidata does not model Montaigne's Essays at a granular level (i.e., books or individual essays), we assign a unique project-specific id to each essay, following the same format for all of them: "b1_01" for the first essay of Book 1, "b3_05" for the fifth essay of Book 3. Since a few entities were not reconciled with an external authority, they are assigned an internal project id: "personb30503", which stands for the third person mentioned in Book 3, Essay 5, who lacks a linked authority id. You can combine all the elements in the entire dataset in one set of nodes and edges tables, as this tutorial does, or you can create a separate set of tables for each essay. Choosing the latter gives you greater flexibility to combine different selections from the dataset, but you need to ensure that you append all the files to the same workspace in Gephi. 

#### Create the edges table

We begin by listing all entity–essay mention relationships (keeping duplicates to preserve frequency) along with their labels, QIDs, and entity type. From this comprehensive table we will derive an edges table and a list of unique nodes (entities and essays). The first column is titled “source” in which you will enter each essay's id; the second is titled “target” in which you will copy the QIDs of the persons and places mentioned in that essay; the third is titled "label", in which you will copy the normalized names of these entities; the fourth is titled "type" (defining entity type) in which you will assign the values "person" or "place". The order in which you add essays is irrelevant, provided that each set of person and place identifiers ("target") is consistently aligned with its corresponding essay identifier ("source"). Multiple mentions are retained as a measure of frequency: for example, if Plato is mentioned five times in Essay b1_01, the table will have five identical rows. After adding all the data, make a copy of the file to use for creating the nodes table. For the edges table, you will delete both the label and entity-type columns and instead add another column called "type", which in this table specifies the edge type with the value "undirected" for all rows. 

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-18.png" alt="Spreadsheet excerpt showing an edges table with repeated essay identifiers in the 'source' column and Wikidata QIDs for person entities in the 'target' column, including duplicate rows representing multiple mentions." caption="Figure 18. Excerpt from the edges table showing people targets from the first two essays of Book 1" %}

#### Create the nodes table

For the nodes table, starting from the copied table of entity–essay mention relationships, you will create four columns: the first one titled “id” contains the Wikidata identifiers for persons and places, to which we will add the project identifiers for essays. In the second column, we retain the “label” column with its values for person and place names and add essay labels in an abridged form to ensure their legibility in the graph. In the third column, we keep the title “type” with its values ("person" and "place") and add a third value—"essay". In the fourth column, we add the attribute "book" for the essays and enter the corresponding book number for each essay. If you decide to use additional attributes, such as occupation for the people, each one becomes a separate column with a value matching each node. Lastly, we remove the duplicate rows, so that each node appears only once. While Gephi can detect and merge duplicate nodes during import, preparing a list with unique nodes simplifies the data input. 

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-19.png" alt="Spreadsheet excerpt showing a nodes table with four columns, 'id', 'label', 'type', 'book', including essay nodes identified by project-specific ids and person and place nodes identified by Wikidata ids." caption="Figure 19. Nodes table showing the labels of the fifteen essay nodes and several person nodes, with their corresponding id and entity type." %}

#### Importing the dataset into Gephi and visualizing the network graph
After downloading [Gephi](https://gephi.org/) (version 0.10 was used in this tutorial), go to *New Project* and select *File* followed by *import spreadsheet* to import first the nodes table and then the edges table. As you are importing the edges file, you need to select the option *Append to existing workspace*. If you prefer to create individual sets of nodes and edges for each essay, this is where you would append each individual spreadsheet as you import it. You can proceed with examining the data in the *Data Laboratory* tab to ensure it was imported correctly and to make any modifications. 

Next, go to the *Overview* tab, which initially shows a graph of dots (nodes) and connecting lines (edges).  After you apply the *Yifan Hu* layout, the nodes will be arranged based on their connectivity into a legible network. Afterwards, you can also apply the *Noverlap* and *Adjust nodes* layouts if you have long labels. From the dark *T* icon on the bottom, you can turn on the node labels, and then modify their font, size, and color with the sliders or the settings icon to the far right. To distinguish node types and emphasize prominent nodes or connections, go to the *Appearance* panel on the left, select the node tab and then the palette icon to define a *Nodes Partition* which allows you to assign different colors to node types or to the weight of the node (e.g., its frequency). Thus, in our graph, essays belonging to the same book are assigned the same color node (Book 1 is pink, Book 2 is blue, Book 3 is gray). In addition, people nodes are colored in purple and place nodes in orange. The size of the nodes can be ranked according to their number of edges by clicking on the second icon to select *ranking* and then *degree*. The last two icons representing label color and label size can also be used to reflect the weight and types of nodes. At the edges tab you can set their weight to appear as a thicker line connecting nodes that are mentioned more often. For example, we can readily observe that the essay on Virgil's poetry has the largest cluster of references, some of which are repeated multiple times and also tend to be mentioned in the other essays. 

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-20.png" alt="Gephi network visualization showing essay nodes colored by book and connected to person and place nodes, illustrating a tripartite network of essays, people, and places." caption="Figure 20. Gephi network graph of the dataset, showing interconnections between individual essay nodes colored according to book and linked to their person and place entities." %}

To the right of the *Graph* workspace in the *Overview* window, you can generate various statistical queries which will then be added to the *Data laboratory* and as choices in the *Appearance* panel. Our dataset includes queries for eigenvector centrality (relative influence based on connectedness), modularity class (defining communities of related nodes), and weighted degree (absolute number of occurrences). The most frequently mentioned person nodes are Plato and Virgil with 29 and Cicero with 28 occurrences, and from the place nodes, the Roman Republic with 15. If we sort the data according to Eigenvector Centrality, this measure privileges Cicero as the most connected figure in the network, followed closely by Lucretius whose weighted degree is significantly smaller at 17; indeed, if we go back to the graph, we can see that Cicero is a central figure, connected to 11 of the essays.  

Our dataset of 15 essays is a relatively small sample from a heterogeneous work with complex intra-textual organization (cf. R. Runyon 2013)[^13], whose richness of references to historical figures and events, geographic locations, and citations, merits comprehensive formal analysis. From our limited network, we can nonetheless observe certain structural tendencies: essays from the same book division tend to cluster together; the geographic locations have fewer connecting nodes than the people, typically contained within the narrative of each essay; Montaigne's cultural matrix is rich and spans wide temporal and geographic distances, but it is clearly anchored in classical antiquity.   

This lesson has followed a workflow for transforming close reading into structured, reusable data without requiring programming skills. By combining semi-automated annotation in Recogito, reconciliation and enrichment in OpenRefine, and network visualization in Gephi, scholars can model textual references as linked entities, explore their relationships beyond linear reading, and publish linked open data datasets that can be reused and extended beyond a single research project.
