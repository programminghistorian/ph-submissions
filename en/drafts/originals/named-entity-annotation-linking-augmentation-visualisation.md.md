## Lesson overview
 The lesson is designed to be accessible to humanities scholars new to digital methods and introduces semi-automated annotation of named entities with Recogito, their linking to Wikidata and enrichment with Wikidata properties in OpenRefine using [Uniform Resource Identifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_Resource_Identifier) (URIs), the creation of new Wikidata items, and network analysis and visualization of named-entity co-occurrences with Gephi. The resulting dataset can be used for more advanced computational exploration of the named entities and their publication as [Linked Open Data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linked_data) (LOD). Michel de Montaigne's _Essays_ are used as the case study, and the same workflow can apply to any literary, historical, or archival corpus. The tutorial can be followed incrementally as a series of self-contained modules, with the output of each feeding into the next stage. To facilitate the modular use of the tools, the lesson is accompanied by a sample dataset, giving readers the option to verify the output of each tool or to enter the workflow at any stage. The tutorial can be complemented with the [introduction to LOD principles](https://programminghistorian.org/en/lessons/intro-to-linked-data) by Blaney and the methodology of building [a gazetteer of places from a historical corpus](https://programminghistorian.org/en/lessons/space-place-gazetteers) by Grunewald and Mostern.

## Introduction

### Linked entities in humanities texts

Named entities, in particular people, places, works, and historical events, are constitutive elements of textual corpora in the humanities, and are often documented in the indexes of print and digital editions as well as in cultural heritage archives. The digital curation of named entities facilitates computational exploration of their relationships, and when these data are published as LOD using the Resource Description Framework (RDF) — a standard model for representing resources and their relationships in machine-readable statements — they can also be linked and reused across datasets on the [Semantic Web](https://www.w3.org/standards/)[^1]. Digital Humanities projects are increasingly adopting graph data models that make the informational content of texts computationally accessible through the semantic annotation of named entities (Spadini et al. 2021).[^2] As Vogeler elaborates (2019),[^3] the methodology for modeling historical texts as "assertive editions" begins with 

>"generic Natural Language Processing steps and then uses Named Entity Recognition to mark up the words representing people, locations, or organizations, temporal data, and quantifying data. The pipeline then relates these entities to one another, building connections between the entities".

The workflow thus combines two complementary modes of textual analysis: the interpretive engagement of *close reading*, which entails editorial judgment in identifying, disambiguating, and verifying entities; and *distant reading* enabled by computational analysis of patterns at corpus scale, which reveals structural features such as clusters, central figures, and cross-references that are not visible through linear reading alone. 

Named Entity Recognition (NER) and Entity-Linking (EL) support the identification and disambiguation of entities by associating textual references with stable identifiers (URIs) in knowledge bases, though automation remains challenging in the humanities due to few gold-standard annotated corpora (Linhares Pontes et al. 2020).[^4] Manual and semi-automated annotation and linking of named entities remain essential in the curation of LOD datasets (Nugues 2024).[^5] Despite the proliferation of digital curation projects, humanities datasets largely lack interoperability (Brown and Simpson 2015;[^6] Hawkins 2021).[^7] The vision of the Semantic Web for “a decentralized database of intertextual relations throughout numerous online editions and archives” (Oberreither 2023:72)[^8] relies on broader scholarly participation across humanities domains. Whether we want to compile a dataset for digitally assisted text analysis, publish a digital edition with structured information, map the geographic locations mentioned in a text, or study an author’s sources and intertexts, the identification, disambiguation, and linking of named entities are fundamental to these research and editorial workflows.

### The sample text and its source document: Michel de Montaigne’s _Essays_ online

Montaigne’s _[Essays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essays_%28Montaigne%29)_ form an eclectic work, rich in quotations and references to historical and fictional persons and events as it weaves a dense intertextual fabric. Although several scholarly digital projects have been dedicated to Montaigne, they have not used linked data to identify and disambiguate named entities.[^9] The dataset developed for this project includes person and place entities reconciled with Wikidata from twenty of Montaigne’s 107 essays and represents a new contribution to Montaigne scholarship; a subset of the data used in this tutorial can be downloaded [here](/assets/named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation). Because Wikidata supports multilingual labels and descriptions while assigning the same identifier to an entity across languages, the dataset can be integrated into existing projects and augmented by other editors. 

The tutorial uses an English translation of the *Essays* downloaded as plain text from Wikisource. The [Wikisource transcription](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Essays_of_Montaigne) was prepared by its volunteer community based on an Optical Character Recognition (OCR) scan of Hazlitt's 1877 edition. To download the essay used in the tutorial — Book I, Essay 2, “Of Sorrow” — navigate to the essay’s [page](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Essays_of_Montaigne/Book_I/Chapter_II), click _Download_, then _Looking for a different format?_, and under _File format_ choose the last option, _Plain text_. Alternatively, download the prepared file, `b1_02.txt`, from the accompanying [lesson dataset](https://github.com/programminghistorian/ph-submissions/tree/gh-pages/assets/named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation). In comparison with [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3600) — the other major community-curated digital library of texts in the public domain — Wikisource has the advantage of providing a separate document for each essay, which facilitates the modular, incremental approach to editing the corpus adopted in this tutorial. Wikisource furthermore has a dynamic editing framework where users can correct the transcription in real time and potentially enrich the text with linked-entity data. 

## From Text to Entities: Named Entity Recognition and annotation with Recogito 

### Getting started with Recogito

[Recogito](https://recogito.pelagios.org/) was developed by the Pelagios network as an open-source semantic annotation platform to support the LOD curation of places. The annotation workspace has a user-friendly graphical interface allowing individuals and teams to easily apply automated Named Entity Recognition to places and persons with Stanford CoreNLP for English, French, Spanish, and German, and to link places to several [gazetteers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gazetteer), such as Pleiades and GeoNames. Recogito also allows for manual annotation of other entities, of relations between tagged entities, free text comments, visualization of places on a digital map, and export of the annotated file in CSV, TEI, Markdown, and RDF formats for further processing and publication. The Recogito website offers multilingual [tutorials](https://recogito.pelagios.org/help); for scholarly discussion of its application to specific case studies, see del Rio Riande (2020)[^10] and Rojas Castro (2025).[^11] This tutorial uses the hosted version of Recogito, accessible by [registering](https://recogito.pelagios.org/signup) for a free user account; alternatively, download the software from GitHub. 

Your first step is to upload your document(s) in .txt UTF-8 or TEI format by clicking on the blue _+New_ button below your username. The tutorial file, `b1_02.txt`, contains Book I, Essay 2, “Of Sorrow,” and was edited to remove Wikisource transcription comments appended at the bottom of the text. You may need to edit your own corpus similarly to remove text that you want excluded from analysis. Once you have uploaded your documents, you will see them listed under _My Documents_ in the left area of the workspace, alongside your available storage (up to 200 MB) and a chronological chart of your editing activity. You can organize your documents into folders and edit the individual document's metadata (author, title, date, source, edition, language, license) from the _Document settings_, which appears as an icon with hover-over description at the top of your workspace alongside _Document view_, _Map view_, _Annotation statistics_, _Download options_.

### Run Named Entity Recognition
To begin the NER annotation of places and people, select your document in the main workspace to activate an _Options_ menu located in the upper right corner with commands to open, duplicate, delete, organize, and run Named Entity Recognition on the document. 

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-01.png" alt="Recogito workspace with a document selected and the Options menu opened, showing commands including Open, Move to, Duplicate, Delete, and Named Entity Recognition." caption="Figure 1. Recogito workspace with the document `b1_02.txt` selected and the *Options* menu opened to show available commands including *Named Entity Recognition*." %}

When you click _Named Entity Recognition_, select the appropriate recognition engine for parsing the text — in this case, the English language model of Stanford CoreNLP. Directly below the *Recognition Engines* is a list of gazetteers for linking your place entities to matching entries. By default, all available gazetteers are selected; if some are particularly relevant to your text’s historical and geographical context, select only those to facilitate the reconciliation process.

### Evaluate the NER annotations and add manual annotations

Evaluating tagged entities requires close reading and may draw on prior familiarity with the text or additional research. NER facilitates discovery of named entities but not without errors. While the automated annotation identified 21 people and 13 places in the sample essay "Of Sorrow," manual review increased the person count to 30 and reduced the place count to 5, revealing both missed entities and false positives. Recogito's automated recognition performs unevenly on multilingual and stylistically complex texts; it therefore functions as a heuristic starting point for editorial close reading and annotation. As an optional extension, you could repeat the NER with a different tool, such as Voyant, and compare the results.[^12]

The *annotation mode* controls appear at the top of the view. Choose *quick mode* to focus on either places or people, or *normal mode* to highlight all annotations by entity type at once. As you click on each annotation, a popup window gives you several options: deleting the annotation, changing its entity type (place, person, event), and adding a custom tag or a comment. If the entity has been recognized as a place and linked to a gazetteer, select *Confirm* or *Change* the automatic match. The *Change* button shows a list of suggestions with a brief description and location on a map, which can be explored further by clicking on an identifier to open the corresponding gazetteer entry. If the correct suggestion is not listed, modify the spelling or add context (e.g., the country for a town) in the _Change_ window's search field.

#### Place annotations

In the sample text, the reference to the Hungarian city of Buda in the sixteenth century is correctly tagged as a place, but GeoNames suggests a town in Romania, even though Hungary is mentioned immediately beforehand in reference to King John of Hungary.

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-02.png" alt="Recogito interface showing an automatically identified place on a map, its gazetteer identifier, and options to confirm, change, or delete the match." caption="Figure 2. Recogito: the automatic match for a place shows a map location, gazetteer id, and options to confirm, change or delete it." %}

To identify the correct place, select *Change* to review the list of suggestions; in this case, the [Kima gazetteer](https://data.geo-kima.org/) correctly identifies the Hungarian city. After selecting it, the annotation is marked as verified and the gazetteer URI is included as a column in the exported CSV file. If you cannot find the correct entity in the available gazetteers, look for a URI (for example, on [Wikidata](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:Main_Page)) and add it manually, or *Flag this place* to complete the reconciliation at a later stage with another tool, as we will do with OpenRefine. After completing the NER vetting and manual annotation, the *Map view* can be used to verify geolocations before exporting the data. 

#### Person annotations

In our edition of Montaigne’s _Essays_, quotations are given in multiple languages along with their English translation, which can result in some words being annotated incorrectly (“Lingua” as a person and “Labitur” as a place); these should be deleted. Conversely, some entities are not recognized, notably the ancient Greek author Sophocles, who must be tagged manually (*+Person*), while the Roman author Ovid is misidentified as a place (Ovid Township in Michigan) and must be deleted and re-tagged as a person. 

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-03.png" alt="Recogito interface showing the name 'Ovid' incorrectly annotated as a place, linked to a GeoNames entry for Ovid Township in Michigan, with a map of that location." caption="Figure 3. Recogito: erroneous annotation of the poet Ovid as a place." %}

It is common to encounter references to people expressed through pronouns or periphrasis. In the sentence mentioning King John of Hungary and Buda (see Figure 2), Recogito tags “John” as a person and “Hungary” as a place, although Hungary is part of John's title, and the entire reference is to “the widow of King John of Hungary”. In our dataset, her name — Isabella Jagiellon — is recorded in the *Add a comment* field to facilitate later reconciliation with Wikidata in OpenRefine. 

As annotations are modified or created, Recogito recognizes other occurrences of the same character sequence and prompts you to apply the annotation to them as well, but each suggestion should be evaluated individually. When creating a new annotation, highlight only the relevant text, as leading punctuation (e.g. “—Seneca”) may be handled correctly in the interface but converted into a question mark in the CSV export.  

#### Event annotations

Recogito also provides an *Event* tag, which is not generated through NER and has to be added manually. In the sample text, the phrase "defeat of Cannae" refers to the [Battle of Cannae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Cannae), while "Cannae" is already automatically recognized as a place and is correctly linked to its entry in the [Pleiades](https://pleiades.stoa.org/home) gazetteer.

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-04.png" alt="Recogito interface showing the place 'Cannae' linked to a Pleiades gazetteer entry with a brief description and a map of its location" caption="Figure 4. Recogito: automatic identification of Cannae as a place, linked to its Pleiades gazetteer entry." %}

To mark the event, highlight the relevant text and click *+Event*. The event annotation partially overlaps the place annotation, allowing the same text string to be represented as both a place and an event in the exported dataset.

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-05.png" alt="Recogito document view showing overlapping annotations on the same text fragment as both a place (highlighted in green) and an event (highlighted in purple)." caption="Figure 5. Recogito: overlapping annotations of the same text fragment as both a place (green) and an event (purple)." %}

#### Custom tags and comments

If additional entity types are relevant, custom tags can be added manually. For texts like Montaigne's *Essays*, where citations are pervasive, tagging works as a distinct entity type makes the intertextual fabric of a text extractable as a layer of structured data. For example, Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* appears as the abbreviation “Met.” in editorial parentheses in the print edition, and so it is incorrectly tagged by Recogito's NER engine as a place. After deleting the place tag, the custom tag “work” can be applied to identify the entity as a creative work, while a comment expanding the abbreviated title can be added in the *Add a comment* field to identify the *Metamorphoses* as the source of Montaigne’s quotation. Custom tags assigned to text selections are stored in the *Add tag* menu for reuse across the document.

In this edition, the editor identifies unattributed quotations in parentheses, typically with author and title, but two editorial references to the _Aeneid_ do not identify its author and were therefore tagged with “Virgil” so that the author can be represented consistently with the other citations. The comment “notes” was further used here to distinguish entities supplied in editorial notes from those occurring in Montaigne’s text. These examples of custom tags and comments illustrate options for preserving additional editorial information in the Recogito workspace; however, works have not been tagged comprehensively in the tutorial's dataset and are not used further in this exercise.

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-06.png" alt="Recogito annotation workspace showing a custom tag and a comment added to a text selection to identify a creative work." caption="Figure 6. Recogito: adding a custom tag and comments to identify a creative work." %}


#### Entity relations

Semantic relations between entities, such as an author and a work or a quotation and its source, can be added in the _Document view_. For example, the relations “isAuthorOf,” “hasAuthor,” or “isPartOf” can be adopted from established RDF vocabularies to describe relationships between bibliographic entities on the Semantic Web. Relations are represented visually by directional arrows indicating their semantic orientation.

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-07.png" alt="Recogito document view showing semantic relations represented by dotted lines with arrows, with one arrow tagged 'isPartOf' going from a quote annotation to a work annotation, and another tagged 'isAuthorOf' going from an annotated person entity to an annotated work entity" caption="Figure 7. Recogito: annotating a relation between a work and its author and between a quote and its source." %}

#### Exporting and preparing annotated data for reconciliation

Recogito's main output for our worked example is a CSV file encoding the entity occurrences by type, which we will subsequently reconcile and enrich in OpenRefine. When you are finished vetting the annotations and adding additional tags and comments, export the data in CSV format using _Download options_ from the icons at the top of the workspace. Recogito produces a single CSV containing all entity types annotated in the document, with 16 columns including identifiers and metadata ([`b1_02_Recogito_export.csv`](https://github.com/programminghistorian/ph-submissions/tree/gh-pages/assets/named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation)). For the next stage of data curation with OpenRefine, only the *entity label*, *entity type*, the *URI for places*, along with any associated *comments* or *tags* that you added need to be retained. Places could be reconciled within Recogito alone for corpora defined by a single period or region, but for Montaigne's *Essays* a single knowledge base spanning periods and entity types is more comprehensive and facilitates data enrichment with additional properties, hence the tutorial's choice to reconcile all entities with Wikidata in the next section.

To proceed with the data curation, you need to open the file downloaded from Recogito as a spreadsheet and sort the rows by _type_ to produce a list of persons followed by a list of places. Copy the persons into a new file and the places into a different file to use during the reconciliation process. Then remove the columns that are not needed in each file: the persons file should retain the columns *quote_transcription* (the annotated text), *tags* and *comments* (if any), while the places file should retain the same columns plus the *URI* column for the place's gazetteer identifier. Before proceeding to the next stage, you should rename the *quote_transcription* columns as `person name` and `place name` respectively and if you have any comments that modify the text in *quote_transcription*, you should use them to normalize the named entity. In our example, the two annotations of “AEneid” tagged “Virgil” are entered as “Virgil” in the _person name_ column, and similarly, the comment Isabella Jagiellon replaces the annotated text “the widow of King John of Hungary”. Entities with spelling or other variations can be normalized during this preparation stage or during reconciliation in OpenRefine. Duplicates are common when the same entity is mentioned multiple times and should be retained for subsequent frequency analysis. Naming files by document and entity type — `b1_02_persons.csv` and `b1_02_places.csv` for Book I, Essay 2 — preserves the modular organization of the corpus and facilitates their later use in constructing the Gephi network. 


## From Entities to Linked Data: knowledge base reconciliation and enrichment of named entities in OpenRefine

[OpenRefine](https://openrefine.org/) is an open-source desktop application with a browser interface. This tutorial uses Version 3.9.3 for knowledge base reconciliation of the dataset; if data normalization is still necessary beforehand, the software [documentation](https://openrefine.org/docs) and the tutorial [Cleaning Data with OpenRefine](https://programminghistorian.org/en/lessons/cleaning-data-with-openrefine) offer such guidance. At this stage, the objective is to link both persons and places to their corresponding entries in Wikidata. The illustration that follows demonstrates the reconciliation of persons; places follow the same workflow, building on the gazetteer matches already obtained in Recogito. As an openly editable and community-maintained resource, Wikidata allows scholars to reuse existing identifiers and to contribute machine-readable descriptions of their research domains where gaps exist. Recent publications in the _Journal of Open Humanities Data_ discuss Wikidata's role in interoperable humanities datasets.[^13]

To get started with OpenRefine, select the _Create project_ tab to upload the prepared persons CSV file ([`b1_02_persons.csv`](https://github.com/programminghistorian/ph-submissions/tree/gh-pages/assets/named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation)), and then select _Next_. A preview screen appears with several parsing options, although OpenRefine usually detects the correct format automatically: in the sample dataset, row 1 is correctly parsed as the column headers “person name” and “comments”. Once the project loads, go to the column labeled *person name* and click the downward arrow. From the dropdown menu, select *Facet* and then *Text facet*. This creates a *Facet* panel on the left, listing unique text values and their counts, which allow you to group and inspect your data and, along with the filters, to exclude selected data during reconciliation. You may need this, for example, if you discover a place or other text string that was incorrectly identified as a person and needs to be removed. Columns can be deleted directly from their dropdown menus, but rows must be removed using filters. Go to the _All_ column and mark the rows you want to delete with stars or flags and select that facet. OpenRefine then sorts the data into *true* and *false* values, with *true* identifying the starred or flagged rows. From the dropdown menu of the *All* column, select *Edit rows* and *Remove matching rows*. Note that OpenRefine tracks every editing step in the *Undo/Redo* panel next to the *Facet/Filter* panel, where you can resume editing from an earlier step.

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-08.png" alt="OpenRefine table displaying a column of person names with a text facet panel on the left listing unique values and frequency counts" caption="Figure 8. OpenRefine: a list of person names defined with a text facet." %}

### Reconcile a list of names with Wikidata

Your next step is to create a new column that duplicates the list of names and stores the reconciliation results separately from the source data. From the *person name* column, open the dropdown menu and select *Edit column*. Then from the second dropdown menu select *Add column based on this column*. In the popup window, write “Wikidata” in the *New column name* box. Begin the reconciliation process by clicking the downward arrow of the new column to select *Reconcile* and then from the next dropdown menu *Start reconciling*. OpenRefine takes you to a screen to select a reconciliation service, where the default option is Wikidata. There, it is possible to connect additional reconciliation services, such as the Virtual International Authority File (VIAF) or the Getty Vocabularies. These provide stable authority identifiers but do not support the same degree of open-ended semantic modeling and scholarly contribution as Wikidata; in fact, these identifiers are often aggregated by Wikidata.

After selecting the Wikidata reconciliation service, a configuration window prompts you to choose an entity class, but OpenRefine will automatically detect a class based on your data (in this case “human”). If your dataset has mixed classes, you could reconcile without specifying one, but the suggestions will be less accurate. To refine reconciliation results, include information from other columns as additional attributes (e.g. “author” or “mythological figure”), allowing OpenRefine to use this contextual information when matching entities. OpenRefine enables the option to auto-match high confidence candidates by default, which is best left selected, since all automatic matches will be reviewed afterwards, just as place name matches were individually vetted in Recogito. When the reconciliation completes, OpenRefine displays a partially colored bar below the “Wikidata” column header, showing the percentage of automated matches with high confidence (27% in the example shown here). These are activated as dark blue links to the corresponding Wikidata page, while entries with suggested matches are displayed as light blue links. If you hover over a link, a brief description from Wikidata appears, and clicking the link will open the full Wikidata entry in a new tab. The hover-over preview makes it easy to verify that Diodorus Siculus, Pliny the Elder, Ovid, and Isabella Jagiellon, among others, are auto-matched correctly.

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-09.png" alt="OpenRefine table of person names showing a reconciled Wikidata column with a progress bar, where high-confidence matches appear as dark blue links and suggested matches appear as light blue links." caption="Figure 9. OpenRefine: reconciled person names distinguishing high confidence matches from suggested matches." %} 

The record “Lesbia”, which in Montaigne's essay appears in a quote by the poet Catullus, is automatically matched to the Wikidata item “Clodia”. However, even though Catullus did use “Lesbia” as a pseudonym for the historical Clodia, the textual context here refers to Lesbia as she appears in his poetry. The match can be refined by selecting *Choose new match* and then *Search for match*. The results now include matches of different class types, and the first suggestion is “Lesbia” described as “lover of Catullus”. Viewing this item on Wikidata shows that it includes the semantic property “said to be the same as,” linking it to the item for Clodia and illustrating how linked data can support semantic disambiguation and contextual specificity. 

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-10.png" alt="OpenRefine 'Search for match' window displaying candidate entities from multiple classes, with 'Lesbia, lover of Catullus' listed as the top result." caption="Figure 10. OpenRefine: refining reconciliation by searching across multiple entity classes." %}

Unreconciled entities may have a ranked list of potential candidates or no matches. Resolving ambiguous or unmatched cases may require returning to the textual context, consulting secondary scholarship, or original research. In the sample reconciliation shown here, “Dionysius the Tyrant” and “Ferdinand I” each return multiple potential matches (see Figure 9 above), while no suggestions are retrieved for "Psammenitus, King of Egypt." For Dionysius the Tyrant, textual context (described as “who died of joy”) identifies him as Dionysius I of Syracuse who appears as the third option. OpenRefine allows reconciliation of either a single cell or all identical cells; here, all occurrences are matched, since the identity is consistent across the text and editorial commentary. For Ferdinand I, the correct match appears under *See more* as Ferdinand I, Holy Roman Emperor, historically in military conflict with Isabella Jagiellon. In cases where no correct match is initially available, *Search for match* can be used to retrieve candidates from different entity classes, as in the case of "Iphigenia", which in this context refers to a mythological figure rather than to the historical persons suggested by default.  

Sometimes spelling variations or aliases prevent correct identification and it may be necessary to modify the original record in the search box. For example, no suggestions appear for "Psammenitus, King of Egypt," because the name is more commonly rendered as “Psamtik”. Searching for “Psamtik” successfully retrieves several Egyptian pharaohs by that name, making possible the identification of Psamtik III based on historical context, and in turn helps disambiguate the identity of related figures, such as Cambyses, King of Persia. 

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-11.png" alt="OpenRefine reconciliation interface showing a manual search for a name's alias in the 'Search for match' field." caption="Figure 11. OpenRefine: modifying the text record alias to search for a Wikidata item." %}

In the sample reconciliation, the only suggestion returned for “Cambyses,” both initially and through _Search for match_, is “Cambyses I”. In such cases, you need to enter the correct historical person in the search box, namely "Cambyses II". If a Wikidata item exists, it will be retrieved, and once the match is selected the Wikidata column will update from “Cambyses, King of Persia” to “Cambyses II”.

If the name search fails to retrieve the correct entry or does not return any results, try searching for it directly in [Wikidata](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:Main_Page). Note that Wikidata items are identified by unique Q-identifiers (QIDs), as in `Q150611` for Ferdinand I. If you find a match, copy its QID and paste it into the search box in OpenRefine, and the relevant entity will be retrieved. 

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-12.png" alt="OpenRefine reconciliation interface showing a Wikidata QID entered directly into the search field to retrieve a specific entity." caption="Figure 12. OpenRefine: linking an entity by adding its Wikidata QID in the *Search for match* field." %}

### Create new Wikidata items

If you cannot match certain entities to existing items in Wikidata, you may consider creating new Wikidata items to describe them.[^14] Although OpenRefine includes an option to create [new Wikidata items](https://openrefine.org/docs/manual/wikibase/new-entities), this tutorial uses Wikidata’s web interface so that the structure of an item and its statements, qualifiers, and references can be examined directly. 

In our sample, no item existed for “Raïsciac, a German lord,” whom Montaigne reports to have died of grief upon seeing the body of his son who was killed in a battle near Buda during the conflict between Ferdinand I and Isabella Jagiellon. The text has enough contextual information to create a structured Wikidata item for this individual. The creation of this new item was done for this tutorial and now exists on Wikidata as [Q135004989](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Q135004989). Because the Raïsciac item already exists on Wikidata, do not recreate it when following this example.

To become a contributor to Wikidata, you should register for a free account with a username and password, and then familiarize yourself with the structure of Wikidata items, especially those corresponding to the type of entity you want to create. For instance, the entry for [Psamtik III](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Q316278) includes a description, aliases, multilingual labels, and a set of structured statements describing attributes, among which dates, gender, political affiliation, occupation, and cause of death, each expressed through properties (P-identifiers) with values often drawn from other Wikidata items (Q-identifiers). Below the *Statements*, the section *Identifiers* links the item to external authority resources, here [*Encyclopædia Britannica*](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Psamtik-III).

To create a Wikidata item, you have to go to [Wikidata’s main page](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:Main_Page) and select *Create a new Item* from the menu. In the window that appears, fill in the basic metadata: language, label, a short description. Once you click *Create* the new item will be assigned a QID. Be careful when creating new items, since deletion requires submitting a request to Wikidata administrators. All modifications are recorded in the item's edit history and are associated with your user profile.

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-13.png" alt="Wikidata interface for creating a new item, showing input fields for language selection, label, short description, and aliases" caption="Figure 13. Wikidata: creating a new item by selecting a language field and entering a label and a short description." %}

The new Wikidata item now has a QID (Q135004989) and you can begin to add statements describing the entity. The first statement asserts *instance of* “human”, which is given the reference *stated in* with the value _Montaigne’s Essays_ (Q530157) and additional references listing the URLs for digital editions and facsimiles of the text. Another relevant statement uses the property *described by source*, again with the value *Montaigne’s Essays*, with *add qualifier* specifying the relevant section (Book I, Essay 2, “Of Sorrow”). Additional statements describe the person's gender (“male”), country of citizenship (“Holy Roman Empire”), occupation (“military personnel”), social classification (“noble”), place of death (“Buda”), and cause of death (“acute stress reaction”). Each of these statements requires explicit references, typically using *stated in* with *Montaigne’s Essays* as the source. Since the date of death is not given explicitly in the text, qualifiers for “earliest date” and “latest date” can be added, and the statement should be referenced as "inferred from" rather than "stated in", since this timeframe is deduced from the historical context of the military conflict between Ferdinand I and Isabella Jagiellon (September 1540—August 1541). Once the narrative evidence has been translated into structured statements, the QID for Raïsciac can be added to our OpenRefine dataset, allowing properties from Wikidata to be imported for all reconciled entities. The live Wikidata item is open to further contributions: Wikidata retains the full edit history of every item, so that structured statements, qualifiers, and references can be expanded, refined, and discussed by contributors over time.

### Extract QIDs and URLs from reconciled data

Once you have reconciled your data, you should extract both QIDs and Wikidata URLs for each matched entity. QIDs are useful for disambiguating, sorting, aggregating, merging duplicates, and referencing entities internally, for example in networks or tables. Wikidata URLs provide stable links to the corresponding Wikidata items and can be used in Linked Data and RDF export. From the drop-down menu of the reconciled Wikidata column select _Reconcile_ and then choose _Add column with URLs of matched entities_. You will be prompted to label the new column (e.g., "Wikidata URLs"), and then it will be added. Repeat the same steps to _Add entity identifiers column_ and label this column "QIDs". The dataset now contains linked identifiers that can be used for further semantic enrichment. After extracting the QIDs for both persons and places, export the reconciled datasets as CSV files, as they will be used to prepare the network data for Gephi in the next section. The completed persons file for the worked example, [`b1_02_persons_OpenRefine_export.csv`](https://github.com/programminghistorian/ph-submissions/tree/gh-pages/assets/named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation), is included in the lesson dataset. You can then continue working in OpenRefine to enrich the dataset with additional Wikidata properties and, optionally, to model it as RDF.

### Enrich the data with Wikidata properties 

OpenRefine can extract additional properties from the reconciled Wikidata column, allowing entities to be grouped, filtered, and compared by characteristics like occupation, gender, citizenship, or life dates, among many others. From the dropdown menu of the reconciled column, select *Edit column* and choose *Add columns from reconciled values*. A window will appear with a short list of suggested properties, a search box to input a property (such as *date of death*), and a preview pane for evaluating whether selected properties contribute useful values. Each property can also be configured by selecting a different choice of ranks and references than the default options. ### Apply the RDF extension and export the dataset

If you intend to publish your dataset as linked data, it must first be modeled using RDF and semantic vocabularies. A recommended lesson at this stage is [Introduction to the Principles of Linked Open Data](https://programminghistorian.org/en/lessons/intro-to-linked-data). Begin the modeling process by experimenting with the RDF extension in OpenRefine. First, install it by going to the main menu for creating a project and then select the option *Extensions*, followed by *Discover extensions* to browse available plugins and download the RDF extension (rdf-extension-1.6.0). Unzip the folder and move it to the extensions directory of your OpenRefine application. The software [documentation](https://openrefine.org/docs/manual/installing#installing-extensions) gives detailed instructions for different operating systems. Once installed, the RDF extension appears in the OpenRefine workspace alongside other extensions (note that Wikibase is pre-installed by default). 

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-17.png" alt="File directory view showing the RDF extension folder placed inside the OpenRefine extensions directory on a Windows system." caption="Figure 17. The RDF extension folder added to the OpenRefine directory on a Windows system." %}

To begin modeling, click the dropdown menu of the RDF extension and select Edit *RDF skeleton*, which opens a window for *RDF Schema alignment*. The RDF Schema alignment is a visual editor where you can assign semantic properties to the columns describing the attributes in the dataset. Select *Add property* to choose a term from the available semantic vocabularies and ontologies (identified by prefixes such as `foaf` and `schema`) that describes the attribute in each column. For instance, `foaf:gender` can be assigned to the column (*Cell*) labeled "sex or gender" extracted from Wikidata. FOAF is the acronym for "Friend of a Friend"—an ontology used to describe people and their relationships in RDF. When matching a semantic property to your list of columns, you will be prompted to define the cell's data type, for example gender and occupation as text, dates of birth and death as dates, and the Wikidata URL as a URI, etc.

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-18.png" alt="OpenRefine RDF Schema alignment window showing the property 'occupation' assigned to a dataset column." caption="Figure 18. OpenRefine: adding the property 'occupation' in the RDF Schema alignment." %}

The RDF skeleton feature of OpenRefine includes several preloaded vocabularies (e.g., `schema, foaf, rdfs, owl`), but you have the option to import others from the [Linked Open Vocabularies](https://lov.linkeddata.es/dataset/lov/) (LOV) platform by clicking *+Add* next to the available prefixes and then typing the name and namespace URI of the desired vocabulary in the box that opens. For instance, you could add the MADS (Metadata Authority Description Schema) to model the property “occupation” in RDF.  

After aligning your data with the appropriate vocabulary terms, you can export the dataset in RDF Turtle format. Publishing the dataset as RDF makes the results of close reading interoperable and reusable beyond a single project, enabling integration with other linked datasets. RDF data modeling is a substantial topic in its own right, and OpenRefine's RDF extension supports lightweight modeling and is best used for becoming familiar with vocabularies and properties. For more complex ontology design, dedicated RDF tools such as [Protégé](https://protege.stanford.edu/), [TopBraid Composer](https://www.topquadrant.com/products/topbraid-composer/), and [GraphDB](https://www.ontotext.com/products/graphdb/) offer richer environments. OpenRefine has an active community of users and developers, and other RDF extensions or workflows may be available as the software evolves.

## From Linked Data to Relations: named entity network analysis and visualization with Gephi

The reconciled named entity dataset can be explored through network analysis and visualization that reveal relational patterns difficult to discern through linear reading alone (cf. [R. Ahnert et al. 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/network-turn/CC38F2EA9F51A6D1AFCB7E005218BBE5)). In this tutorial, network visualization allows us to examine some aspects of how Montaigne’s discourse is structured through his historical and cultural references to persons and places. We will create a tripartite network of essays, people and places from the first five essays of each book. The network represents co-occurrences across the fifteen essays, revealing degrees of interconnection among entities and suggesting patterns in the text's formal organization within this limited selection. Modeling works as a third named-entity type would introduce an important semantic layer of intertextual relations and is a natural extension of the workflow: a reader who carried out comprehensive works tagging in Recogito (see the "Custom tags and comments" section above) could reconcile those entries with Wikidata in OpenRefine, then add them to the Gephi network. 

### Setting up the dataset for network analysis with Gephi 

[Gephi](https://gephi.org/) is a well-documented, open-source desktop application for network visualization and analysis that provides interactive layouts and statistical measures by default, allowing users to explore relationships in their dataset without programming skills. Gephi is currently supported on Windows 10 and later, MacOS, Apple Silicon, and Linux. Gephi imports data as tables of nodes and edges, so we create two CSV files: the nodes table lists each node and its ID; the edges table defines relationships between the nodes. In the nodes table you can keep any attribute columns you exported from Wikidata with OpenRefine or that you added in Recogito, such as gender, country, occupation, editorial note. However, such data is easier to explore statistically, and too many attributes will become difficult to work with in a network visualization. For the basic tripartite network with Gephi, we will explore the relationship between named entities and their occurrences in the selected essays, excluding any attributes. Thus, since Virgil is mentioned in Book I, Essay 2, this mention becomes an edge in the network, where Virgil is a node with type "person" and Book I, Essay 2 is a node with type "essay". In the edges table, Book I Essay 2 is described as the "source" and Virgil as the "target", and a "type" column specifies whether each edge is directed or undirected (see Table 1 below, where every edge is set to undirected). In this network we model co-occurrences without modeling derivation or influence, so all edges are undirected. A directed network would be appropriate for a corpus where the direction of reference is meaningful — for example, modeling citations between multiple authors, where it matters that author A cites author B and not vice versa. Using Wikidata QIDs as node IDs anchors the network in a shared knowledge base: this disambiguates entries that share a name (such as Pliny the Elder and Pliny the Younger), normalizes spelling variations across the corpus, and makes it possible to incorporate additional Wikidata properties, including dates, occupations, and citizenships, as node attributes for filtering and analysis, should you wish to extend the analysis. 

To prepare the nodes and edges tables from the OpenRefine exports, we copy the column with Wikidata-normalized names for the labels and use the matching QIDs as unique identifiers for the nodes. Wikidata does not model Montaigne's *Essays* at a granular level (i.e., books or individual essays), therefore we assign a unique project-specific ID to each essay, following the same format: "b1_01" for the first essay of Book I, "b3_05" for the fifth essay of Book III. Since a few entities were not reconciled with an external authority, they are similarly assigned an internal project ID: "personb30503" which stands for the third unreconciled person mentioned in Book III, Essay 5. You have the option to combine the files of all fifteen essays into one set of nodes and edges tables, as this tutorial does, or to create a separate set for each essay. Choosing the latter gives you greater flexibility to combine different selections from the dataset for more targeted queries, but you need to ensure that you append all the files to the same workspace in Gephi. The two nodes and edges tables used in this section, `Montaigne_nodes_Gephi.csv` and `Montaigne_edges_Gephi.csv`, can be [downloaded](https://github.com/programminghistorian/ph-submissions/tree/gh-pages/assets/named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation) if you wish to enter the workflow at the network visualization step.

#### Create the edges table

The nodes and edges tables are prepared in a spreadsheet (in our case, Excel), using the per-essay CSVs produced during the OpenRefine reconciliation as the source data. We begin by listing all entity–essay mention relationships (keeping duplicates to preserve frequency) along with their labels, identifiers, and entity type. From this comprehensive table we will derive an edges table and a list of unique nodes (entities and essays). The table needs four columns. The first one is called *source*, where you enter each essay's ID. The second is called *target*, where you copy the QIDs of the persons and places mentioned in that essay, or the project-specific identifiers for unreconciled entities. In the third, called *label*, copy the normalized names of these entities. Lastly, in *type*, assign the values "person" or "place". The order of adding essays is irrelevant, provided that each identifier in *target* is consistently aligned with its essay identifier in *source*. Multiple mentions are retained as a measure of frequency: for example, if Plato is mentioned five times in Essay b1_01, the table will have five identical rows. After adding all the data, make a copy of the file to use for creating the nodes table. For the edges table, delete the *label* and *type* (entity type) columns, leaving the *source* and *target* columns, and add a *type* column with the value “undirected” for every row — this one specifies edge direction rather than entity type.

#### Create the nodes table

For the nodes table, starting from the copied table of entity–essay mention relationships, you will create four columns: the first one titled “id,” contains the QIDs for reconciled persons and places and project-specific identifiers for unreconciled entities and essays. In the second column, we retain the “label” column with the normalized person and place names and add abridged essay labels to ensure their legibility in the graph. In the third column, we keep the title “type” with its values ("person" and "place") and add a third value — "essay". In the fourth column, we add the attribute "book" for the essays and enter the book number for each essay. If you decide to use additional attributes, such as occupation for the people, each one becomes a separate column with a value matching each node. Lastly, remove duplicate rows so that each node appears only once, using the *Remove Duplicates* button on the *Data* ribbon. While Gephi can detect and merge duplicate nodes during import, preparing a list with unique nodes simplifies the data input.

#### Importing the dataset into Gephi and visualizing the network graph
After downloading [Gephi](https://gephi.org/) (version 0.11.3 was used in this tutorial), go to *New Project* and select *File* followed by *Import Spreadsheet* to import first the nodes table and then the edges table. When importing the edges file, select *Append to existing workspace* to add the edges to the previously imported nodes. If you choose to use individual files for each essay rather than combining all entities into one, this is where you would append each individual spreadsheet as you import it.  The next step is to examine the data in the *Data Laboratory* tab to ensure that it was imported correctly and to make any modifications if you notice errors. 

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-19.png" alt="Gephi 0.11.3 edge import window showing the graph type set to Undirected and the option Append to existing workspace selected." caption="Figure 19. Importing the edges table in Gephi 0.11.3. Select *Undirected* as the graph type and *Append to existing workspace* to add the edges to the previously imported nodes." %}

Next, go to the *Overview* tab, where you will see a tangled graph of dots (nodes) and connecting lines (edges). In the *Layout* panel, select *Yifan Hu*, a force-directed algorithm that positions nodes according to their connectivity, and click *Run*. Stop the layout when the network reaches a legible arrangement. Depending on the nature of your dataset, other algorithms, such as Force Atlas 2, may be more appropriate. After adding labels, try also applying *Noverlap* and *Label Adjust* to make a graph with a lot of text more legible.

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-20.png" alt="Gephi 0.11.3 Overview showing the network after applying the Yifan Hu layout, with the Yifan Hu controls visible in the Layout panel." caption="Figure 20. The network after applying the *Yifan Hu* layout." %}

To display node labels, select *Node Label* in the controls below the graph and check *Show*. The *Configure* menu allows you to choose which attribute is displayed; here we use the node label. The font and scale of the labels can be adjusted with a slider or by selecting them from the dropdown menus. The visualization of the network can then be modified from the *Appearance* panel. To distinguish the three node types, select *Nodes*, choose *Partition*, and partition the nodes by *type*, assigning a different color to each category from the preset menu. In our graph, essay nodes are green, person nodes purple, and place nodes orange. Node size can be used to emphasize more frequently occurring entities: select the node size control (visible in hover-over menu), choose *Ranking*, and select *Weighted Degree*. Then set the minimum and maximum sizes to 10 and 50 and click *Apply*. The *Appearance* panel controls also modify label color and size, matching the color of the nodes or all uniform; in our graph, the labels are all black. Under *Edges*, you can similarly adjust the appearance of the connections, including their thickness according to edge weight, so that repeated relationships can appear more prominently. Thus, in the resulting graph, the essay on Virgil's poetry forms the largest cluster of references, while the differently colored person and place nodes make it possible to distinguish the kinds of entities connected to each essay.

The complete graph gives an overview of the dataset, but individual relationships can be examined more closely by selecting a node. When you click on an individual node, its connections are highlighted while the rest of the network fades into the background, making it easier to trace that node's relationships across the graph. Selecting Plutarch, for example, reveals the nine essays where he is mentioned and makes his cross-essay connections easier to follow.

{% include figure.html filename="en-or-named-entity-annotation-linking-augmentation-visualisation-22.png" alt="Gephi network with the Plutarch node selected and its connections to several essay nodes highlighted while the rest of the network is faded." caption="Figure 22. Selecting Plutarch highlights the essays in which he appears and makes his connections across the selected corpus visible." %}

The *Statistics* panel to the right of the *Graph* workspace provides additional measures for exploring the network. When you run a measure, Gephi automatically adds these values as new attributes in the *Data Laboratory* and makes them available for use in the *Appearance* panel. Among the measures useful for this dataset, eigenvector centrality assigns higher values to nodes connected to other highly connected nodes; modularity class identifies communities of densely interconnected nodes; and weighted degree takes the weights of connected edges into account. In this network, weighted degree is especially useful for distinguishing entities mentioned repeatedly from those that occur only once, while measures such as eigenvector centrality can help identify figures that connect multiple parts of the network. 

As we can verify in the *Data Laboratory*, the most frequently mentioned person nodes are Plato and Virgil with 29 occurrences each and Cicero with 28; among the place nodes, the Roman Republic has 15. Sorting the data by eigenvector centrality produces a different picture: Cicero has the highest value, followed closely by Lucretius, even though Lucretius has a substantially lower weighted degree of 17. This is because weighted degree reflects the frequency of mentions, whereas eigenvector centrality also takes into account how well connected a node's neighbors are. Cicero occurs across 11 of the fifteen essays, while Lucretius appears across 8, including several of the more highly connected essay nodes. Thus, weighted degree reflects absolute frequency, while eigenvector centrality reflects a node's relative prominence within the network. 

Our dataset of fifteen essays is a relatively small sample from a heterogeneous work with complex intra-textual organization (cf. R. Runyon 2013)[^15], whose richness of references to historical figures and events, geographic locations, and citations merits comprehensive formal analysis. From our limited network, we can nonetheless observe certain structural tendencies: essays from the same book division tend to cluster together through shared named entities; the place nodes have fewer connections than the person nodes and are typically contained within the narrative of each essay; Montaigne's cultural matrix is rich and spans wide temporal and geographic distances, but it is clearly anchored in classical antiquity.   

This lesson has followed a workflow for transforming close reading into structured, reusable data without requiring programming skills. By combining semi-automated annotation in Recogito, reconciliation and enrichment in OpenRefine, and network visualization in Gephi, scholars can model textual references as linked entities, explore their relationships as a graph, and publish linked open data datasets that can be reused and extended beyond a single research project.
